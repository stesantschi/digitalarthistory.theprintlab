Start where your question is

This page sorts the modules by what you want to do.

Find the description closest to your own question and start there. The modules can be read in any order; the prerequisites on each module page say what its examples assume.

  • I want to understand how online collections are actually made and managed

    Start with module 2, which takes apart a record and asks what each field is doing. Then module 1 for what happens to names when a collection is assembled, and module 5 for how institutions publish their data to each other.

    The short answer to the underlying question: a collection is made by people making choices, and almost none of those choices are recorded anywhere a user can see.

  • I want to build my own database for the material I work on

    Module 1 is the foundation, and its concordance is the thing you will use every day afterwards. Module 2 tells you which fields to have and why. Module 5 covers identifiers, reconciliation against Japanese authorities, and how to publish so that other people's work can meet yours.

    Read module 5's step 6 before you create anything anywhere. The four options for where a new entry belongs are the decision that shapes everything after it.

  • I want to make an online exhibition, and I have not built a website before

    Module 6, which ends by building one from a spreadsheet using a template that needs nothing installed. Read its step 2 first, on who decides the order and how the text meets the image, because those two questions decide the tool.

    If you have written a wall label or worked on an exhibition, you already have the harder half of this. What is new is what the digital medium decides for you before you begin.

  • I work with bound books and want to display them properly

    Module 6, and specifically the six conditions. A book asks things of a display tool that most tools were not built to give, and the first step is being able to say what those things are.

  • I cannot read early modern Japanese, and I need to identify a book anyway

    Identifying a book you cannot read — a short guide on the routes that do not depend on reading, and on what each one lets you claim afterwards.

    This is the commonest situation in the room and not a deficiency. Most identification work is done on format, structure, marks and matching rather than on reading.

  • I want to compare images across collections at scale

    Module 4. It measures visual similarity, shows you what the measurement actually responded to, and is candid that a general model has seen almost no Japanese prints.

    Read its step 3 before forming any interpretation. Clusters that track the photographing institution rather than the picture are the normal first result.

  • My material is spatial — sites, architecture, how something was seen

    Module 3, with a caveat: it works on where a picture is of and where it was seen from, across a landscape. Relationships inside a building, or between an object and its setting, need different methods, and its step 5 on viewpoints is the part that transfers.

  • My period is modern, and early modern is background

    Module 2 and module 6 transfer directly — records disagree and displays decide things regardless of century. The material specifics in the green margin tabs are the part to read slowly.

  • I want to teach this to my own students

    Everything here is CC BY 4.0 and the source is on GitHub. Take a module, cut it, translate it, put your own material through it. Attribution is the only condition.

    The most portable pieces are the six conditions in module 6 and the four checks in module 2. Both are one page and both work on any material.

What is not here yet

Stated plainly, because a resource that quietly lacks something wastes more of your time than one that says so.

  • Building and publishing as a subject in its own right. It is currently one step at the end of module 6, and it is the thing most people ask for first. A module of its own is the next thing to be written.
  • 3D capture and reproduction. Photogrammetry, scanning, printing — nothing here covers it. The nearest adjacent work is in conservation and museum-technology literature rather than in this practice book.
  • A survey of existing digital projects in the field. What has been built, what worked, where the limits turned out to be. Worth having, not yet written.
  • Whether digital curation complements a physical exhibition or replaces it. The sharpest question anyone can ask about this field, and the one this site currently walks past. Module 6's two axes are the beginning of an answer rather than the answer.
  • Reading cursive script. Deliberately out of scope; it is a separate subject taught separately. The guide above covers working without it.

If something you need is missing, say so through the repository issues. This list is how the next modules get chosen.
