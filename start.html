Start where your question is

Find the question closest to yours and follow the link. The modules can be read in any order.

New to digital methods? Read Models, maps and counting first. Each module page lists what its examples assume.

  • I want to understand how online collections are made and managed

    Start with module 2, which reads a catalogue record field by field. Then module 1, on what happens to names when a collection is assembled, and module 3, on how institutions publish their data to each other.

    In short: people make a collection by making choices, and the record shows few of those choices.

  • I want to build my own database for the material I work on

    Start with module 1: its concordance of names is the tool you will use most. Module 2 shows which fields to include and why. Module 3 covers identifiers, matching your names to Japanese authority files, and publishing so that your data connects with other people's.

    Read module 3's step on contributing data before you create any entries. Where a new entry belongs is the decision that shapes everything after it.

  • I want to make an online exhibition, and I have not built a website before

    Go to module 7. It ends by building an exhibition from a spreadsheet, with a template that needs no installation. Read its step 2 first: who decides the order, and how the text meets the image. Those two questions decide the tool.

    If you have written a wall label or worked on an exhibition, you already have the curatorial skills. The module adds what the digital medium decides for you.

  • I work with bound books and want to display them well

    Go to module 7 and its six conditions: what a book needs from a display tool, stated before you choose one.

  • I cannot read early modern Japanese, and I need to identify a book

    Read Identifying a book you cannot read. It gives four routes that work without reading the text, and what each lets you claim.

    Many people who work with these books are in this situation. Most identification uses format, structure, marks and image matching.

  • I want to compare images across collections at scale

    Go to module 5. It measures visual similarity and shows what the measurement responded to. General models have seen few Japanese prints, and the module shows what follows from that.

    Read its step 3 before interpreting anything. The first groups usually follow the institution that photographed the prints.

  • I want to use an AI model to describe or identify images

    Go to module 6. It sets up a test: an answer key written before you ask, a score for every answer, and a check of your own judgement against a second reader.

    Write the answer key first. Once you have read the model's answers, your key will drift towards them.

  • My material is spatial: sites, architecture, how something was seen

    Go to module 4. It covers where a picture is of and where it was seen from, across a landscape. For relationships inside a building, or between an object and its setting, its step 5 on viewpoints applies; the rest needs other methods.

  • My period is modern, and early modern is background

    Module 2 and module 7 apply to any period: records disagree, and displays make decisions. Read the Japanese-material tabs closely for the period-specific details.

  • I want to teach this to my own students

    Everything here is CC BY 4.0, and the source is on GitHub. Take a module, shorten it, translate it, use your own material. Attribution is the only condition.

    The six conditions in module 7 and the four questions in module 2's worked case each fit on one page and work with any material.

Planned, and out of scope

  • Building and publishing. Currently one step at the end of module 7. It is what people ask for most, and a module of its own is next.
  • A survey of existing digital projects in the field: what has been built, what worked, where the limits were. Planned.
  • Digital and physical exhibitions: how they complement each other. Module 7's two axes give a first answer; a fuller one is planned.
  • 3D capture and reproduction (photogrammetry, scanning, printing). Out of scope; see the conservation and museum-technology literature.
  • Reading cursive script. Out of scope: it is a subject of its own. The guide on identifying books covers working without it.
