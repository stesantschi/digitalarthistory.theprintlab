Models, maps and counting

Three ideas the modules rest on, explained for art historians.

  • ~20 min
  • no prerequisite
  • CC BY 4.0

The modules use a few words that mean different things in art history and in computing. This page says how the site uses three of them: model, map, and the pair quantitative and qualitative.

What a model is

A model is a simplified stand-in for something, made for a purpose. It keeps some features and leaves out others, and the choice of what to keep is the model's argument. Scholars use models to think with: a reconstruction, a diagram, a scale model each let you try out ideas that the thing itself does not (Morgan and Morrison 1999).

You will meet three meanings:

  • A model to copy: an example a pupil works from.
  • A model as representation: a reconstruction or diagram that stands in for something too large, too distant or too complex to handle directly.
  • A computational model: a program whose internal settings were adjusted on many examples until it reproduces the patterns in them. Asked about a new case, it answers by analogy with what it was trained on.

All three share one consequence. A model's answer comes from a particular stand-in, so ask what it was built from, for what purpose, and what it leaves out. The statistician George Box put it plainly: every model is wrong, and the useful question is whether it is useful (Box 1976).

The Japanese material

Tehon (手本) means a model to copy by hand: calligraphy copybooks, and painting manuals (etehon) such as Hokusai's. Learning by copying a model was the ordinary way to learn an art.

This site takes its name from them. A tehon was a teaching tool, made for learning. A computational model is also made for a purpose, and its answers serve that purpose.

The computational operation

An AI model is a very large set of numbers (its parameters), fixed during training on a collection of examples (its training data). A vision-language model (VLM) takes an image and a question and writes an answer.

The same model name can cover several versions that answer differently. Record the name, the version and the date of every result.

What a map is

A map is a model of space. It selects, simplifies and arranges; it has a scale, an orientation and a purpose. No map shows everything, and each one answers a question someone asked. Maps are therefore arguments as well as records (Harley 1989).

Two kinds matter for these modules:

  • A map that measures: places at positions in coordinates, distances to scale.
  • A map that shows meaning: routes, famous places, sequences of travel, where order and association matter more than distance.

When the modules "map" prints, they place them in coordinates: a map that measures. Module 4 explains what that keeps and what it loses.

The Japanese material

Many Edo-period maps combine both kinds: pictorial views, place names and routes, with sequence and association often more important than measured distance. Printed maps and illustrated guidebooks circulated widely (Yonemoto 2003).

A landscape print sits close to the second kind. Plotting it on the first kind is a translation, and module 4 is about that translation.

The computational operation

A geographic information system (GIS) stores places as points, lines or areas, each with coordinates in a stated coordinate system. A basemap is a modern map placed under your data for orientation.

Everything entered into a GIS takes the form of the first kind of map, whatever kind of map it came from.

Quantitative and qualitative research

Qualitative research interprets cases in depth: what this print means, how it was made, whom it was for. Its evidence is close looking, texts and context. Its strength is depth; its open question is how typical the case is.

Quantitative research counts and measures across many cases: how often, how many, how closely two things go together. Its strength is scale and pattern; its open question is whether the things counted were defined well.

The two depend on each other. Every count starts with a qualitative decision about what to count, such as whether a print is a "landscape". Every pattern found by counting needs interpretation before it means anything. Data in the humanities are selected: someone chose what to record and how (Drucker 2011).

The usual bridge between the two is coding: reading cases and labelling them with categories from a written list (a codebook), then counting the labels (Saldaña 2021). Three habits make the counts trustworthy:

  1. Record how sure you are

    For each judgement: certain, probable or possible. The same distinction art history already makes with "signed", "attributed to" and "circle of".

  2. Ask a second person to code the same cases

    How far you agree shows how stable the categories are. High agreement and low agreement are both findings.

  3. Report the denominator and a case

    Write "9 of 125" rather than "7%", and pair each number with a worked example that shows what it counts.

The computational operation

Agreement between coders is measured with scores such as Cohen's kappa or Krippendorff's alpha, which correct for the agreement two coders would reach by chance. A score of 0 means chance level; 1 means complete agreement (Krippendorff 2019).

For small numbers, give a range as well as a figure: 9 of 125 is about 7%, with a plausible range of roughly 4 to 13%.
