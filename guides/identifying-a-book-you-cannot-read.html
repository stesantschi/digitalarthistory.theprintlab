Identifying a book you cannot read

Four routes that do not depend on reading, and what each one lets you claim afterwards.

  • ~30 min
  • no prerequisite
  • browser only
  • CC BY 4.0

Most people working with early modern Japanese books cannot read them fluently, including many who work with them professionally. This is the ordinary condition of the field rather than a gap to apologise for, and a great deal of identification work does not require reading at all.

What follows is four routes. None of them is a substitute for reading, and each one produces a claim of a different strength. Knowing which strength you have is the point.

The Japanese material

An early modern Japanese book carries a great deal of identifying information in places that are not prose: the format and binding, the position and shape of the title slip, the publisher's mark, seals, and the volume marker printed in the gutter.

Someone who cannot read a sentence can often read all of these, because they are positional and formal rather than linguistic.

Look before you search

Before any database, describe what is in front of you. Six things, none of which needs reading.

  1. 01Format and binding

    Size, proportion, how it is sewn, whether the leaves are folded. This narrows the field of what the book can be more sharply than most people expect.

  2. 02Where the title slip sits, and what shape it is

    Its position and form are conventional, and the conventions changed. You do not have to read it to record it.

  3. 03The gutter marker

    Many books number their openings in the fold, often with a volume indicator — the kind of mark that reads 下ノ二, meaning the second opening of the lower volume. Two characters, learnable in an afternoon, and they tell you where you are in a set.

  4. 04Marks and seals

    Publisher's marks, collectors' seals, censor seals. Each is a shape you can photograph, compare and search on, and several of them are datable independently of anything written.

  5. 05The colophon's position and shape

    Even unread, whether a colophon is present, where it sits and how much of it there is tells you something — and it tells you where to point a transcription tool later.

  6. 06How many volumes, and does this copy have them all

    The commonest reason a search fails is that you are looking for a set and holding a part.

Write these down before searching. They are what you will match against, and they are also the part of your eventual record that rests on observation rather than on somebody's say-so.

Match the image, not the text

A page is a picture, and pictures can be searched by picture. This works when a title search has failed, which for this material is often.

  • Reverse image search on a full page or a distinctive detail. General search engines do this, and so do some subject aggregators for prints.
  • Your six observations from route 1 as search terms: format plus approximate date plus subject will often surface a candidate where a title will not.
  • The illustration itself. Blocks were reused, recut and copied. A design appearing in two books is a finding rather than a coincidence, and it is exactly the kind of thing image matching is good at.
How to read a match

A visual match tells you two images resemble each other. It does not tell you the books are the same edition, the same state, or even the same work — a design reissued decades later under a new title will match perfectly.

So treat a match as a candidate to check, not as an identification. What it has genuinely given you is a title to search on, which is what you did not have.

Use a union catalogue rather than a library catalogue

This is the route most people miss, and it is usually the fastest.

A library catalogue tells you what one institution holds, under the title that institution chose. A union catalogue works the other way: it clusters variant titles onto one work record, so a search that fails everywhere else can succeed there. For early modern Japanese books, the union catalogue of Japanese texts maintained at the National Institute of Japanese Literature is the place to start when a title search has failed.

The computational operation

The difference is a data model. A library catalogue is organised around holdings: one record per copy in one building. A union catalogue is organised around works, with titles as attributes of the work rather than as its identity.

That is why a variant title finds the work in one and finds nothing in the other. It is the same distinction an authority file makes for names, applied to titles.

Once you have the work record, you have the cluster of titles the book has been known by — and that cluster is what you take back to the museum and library catalogues that failed you.

Also worth trying, in this order

Artist plus date range. Where a title is unreliable, an attribution and a decade often are not.

Publisher. Firms are datable and their output is finite.

Illustrator rather than author, and the reverse. Catalogues disagree about which of the two goes in the main field, so searching only one halves your reach.

Let a machine read, and treat what it says as a lead

Text recognition for cursive Japanese exists and has improved considerably. A general large model will also offer a reading of a page. Both are useful and both belong at a particular point in the process.

The right use is generating search terms. A machine reading of a title slip gives you candidate readings, alternative romanisations, and a set of characters to paste into a catalogue. You then verify against a record made by a person, and it is the record that becomes your source.

Caution

The wrong use is treating the output as a transcription. A model returns a fluent reading whether or not it recognised anything, and it will not tell you which characters it was unsure about. On cursive script, on a damaged sheet, or on a term specific to a trade, the failure looks exactly like the success.

The practical rule: a machine reading may point you to a claim. It may not be the evidence for one. If a date reached your record through a machine transcription that nobody has checked, that is a different kind of claim from a date read off a colophon, and your record should say which it is.

The Japanese material

Models trained on cursive script are trained on particular hands, periods and genres. Performance on a printed literary text says little about performance on a trade manual, a publisher's advertisement, or a page where the block was worn.

Where a reading matters to your argument, this is the point at which to ask someone who reads the script. That is a normal step in the work, not a failure of the tools.

What each route lets you claim

The whole point of separating them.

RouteWhat you can say afterwards
LookingObservation. The strongest thing you have, and the only one that comes from the object.
Image matchingA resemblance, and a candidate to check. Not an identification.
Union catalogueWhat a cataloguing body has concluded, with its own basis, which you can go and look at.
Machine readingA lead. Cite the record you verified it against, never the model.
The question this connects to

The question worth asking of any date in a record is whether it is on the object. If you cannot read the colophon, you cannot answer that directly — and can't tell is a real answer, common among people who read the script perfectly well.

What these four routes let you do is say something more useful than can't tell: which route the claim reached you by, and therefore how far it is from the sheet. A date that arrives from a union catalogue record is at a stated distance from the object. A date that arrives from an unverified machine reading is at an unknown one.

This is module 2's subject, met from the other side.

What this cannot do

  • It cannot replace reading. Argument that turns on what a text says needs someone who can read it, and knowing when you have reached that point is part of the skill.
  • It cannot settle an edition or a state. Those turn on comparing copies, which needs the copies.
  • It cannot identify what was never catalogued. Every route except looking depends on somebody having described the book already.
