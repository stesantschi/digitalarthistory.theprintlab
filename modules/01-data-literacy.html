Module 01

Data literacy for Japanese datasets

Names and lists — what your data already decided before you opened it.

  • ~60 min
  • no prerequisite
  • your own spreadsheet, or the example
  • CC BY 4.0

Outline. The worked example and its code are being prepared; this page gives the question, the steps and their limits.

The research question

Starting point

I have a spreadsheet of two hundred prints I gathered from four sources. Half the artist names don't match each other. How do I turn this into one list?

This is the first thing that happens to everyone, and it looks like a tidying job. It is not. Each mismatch is a place where somebody made a decision — about how to romanise a name, about which of an artist's names to record, about whether a work has one maker — and the decisions were made by different people, in different institutions, for different purposes. Tidying the list means choosing whose decision wins.

The Japanese material

Japanese names appear family-name-first in Japanese and often given-name-first in Western catalogues, and the two are not reliably marked.

Long vowels can be written with a macron (Ryōko), a doubled vowel (Ryouko), an h (Ohno), or nothing at all (Ryoko). All four turn up in real records.

Beyond that, an Edo print designer worked under an art name, a gō, and changed it across a career. Hokusai signed as Shunrō, Sōri, Taito, Iitsu and others. Those are not variants of one name; they are different names for one person.

The computational operation

Matching two strings is an identity test, and it uses a criterion: two names are the same when their characters are the same. That is a defensible criterion. It is not the one you use.

A cataloguer identifies by signature, seal, date and context. Character equality identifies by typographic form. Everything in this module is the work of translating the first criterion into terms the second can carry, one decision at a time — and each translation loses something you should be able to name.

What we will do

Open a messy list and read what is in it. Count the variants of each name. Normalise the ones that are the same string written differently. Then build a concordance by hand for the ones that are different names for the same person, because no algorithm can do that part.

Step 1 — Open it and look

Before any cleaning, find out what the file actually contains.

The computational operation

Encoding is how text was stored as numbers by whoever exported the file. Modern files use Unicode; older Japanese ones often use Shift-JIS, which does not travel.

Get it wrong and Japanese arrives as boxes: 文字化け, mojibake. Try encoding="shift_jis" or encoding="cp932" before concluding the file is broken.

Worth noting as a matter of evidence: the encoding is a property of the export, not of the material. It tells you something about the institution's systems and nothing about the print.

Two artist columns, which is already informative: somebody decided the Latin-script and Japanese-script forms were different fields. Whether they are populated consistently is the next question.

How to read this

Missing values cluster by source rather than scattering evenly, and that pattern is the finding. One institution fills the Japanese-script field and another leaves it empty, because one catalogues in Japanese and the other transliterates on entry.

A count of missing values is therefore a description of four cataloguing practices, before it is a description of your data.

Step 2 — Count the variants

The Japanese material

Katsushika is a place name adopted as a surname; Hokusai is the gō. The pairing is a convention of Western cataloguing rather than how he signed.

A record giving only Hokusai is not incomplete in the same way a record giving only Katsushika would be. The gō is the identifying part.

Step 3 — Normalise, and notice what it costs

Normalisation makes strings comparable by removing differences you have decided do not matter. The phrase to hold on to is you have decided.

The computational operation

Normalisation is an argument about which differences are meaningless, applied at scale and then made invisible.

Unicode stores some characters more than one way: full-width Ａ and half-width A look almost identical and are different characters. NFKC is one of four standard ways of folding those together, and it is aggressive — it also turns ① into 1 and ㍿ into a company suffix.

Choosing it commits you to the position that those distinctions carry no evidence in your material. Sometimes true. Worth stating rather than inheriting.

Choice point

How far do you normalise? Each option loses something.

Whitespace and case only
Safe, reversible, and leaves most of the problem unsolved. A good default when the dataset is going to be published.
Add Unicode NFKC folding
Resolves full-width and half-width forms, which is usually right for names. Also flattens distinctions in other columns, so apply it per column rather than to the whole table.
Strip macrons and diacritics
Matches Ryōko to Ryoko, and also matches things that should stay apart. It discards information the record was careful to record.
Lowercase everything
Convenient for matching, unhelpful for display. Keep the original column and match on a derived one.

The rule worth following: normalise into a new column, never over the original. The original is your evidence.

Step 4 — Build the concordance by hand

What is left after normalisation is the part that needs a person: names that are genuinely different and refer to the same maker.

How to read this

The unmatched list is the working list, and it should shrink slowly. A concordance that covers everything on the first pass usually means the equivalences were asserted too freely.

Fuzzy matching, which scores how similar two strings look, will offer to do this automatically. It measures typographic distance and knows nothing about people, so it will happily merge two artists with similar names and refuse to merge Shunrō with Hokusai. Use it to propose candidates, never to decide them.

What this cannot tell you

  • It cannot tell you that two names are one person. That is an art-historical judgement, drawn from signatures, dates, seals and the literature. The concordance records the judgement; it does not make it.
  • It cannot date a name change. Knowing that Shunrō became Hokusai is one thing; knowing when, and therefore which prints fall on which side, is a scholarly question with a contested answer.
  • It cannot see what was never recorded. A block cutter absent from every record in your four sources is absent from your analysis, and the absence will look like a fact about Edo publishing.
  • It cannot tell you which source to trust. When two records disagree about a name, the concordance forces you to choose, and the choice is yours to defend.

What you have at the end is a list that a computer can count, plus a written record of every decision that made it countable. The second half is the part that lets someone else check your work.

Use your own materials

  1. Replace the example file with your own export. Any CSV with a name column will do.
  2. Run steps 1 and 2 unchanged. The variant count is usually the moment the scale of the problem becomes clear.
  3. Write your own concordance, starting from the variants the count returns. Include the third column from the first line.

Keep the concordance in your project as a file in its own right. It is reusable across every later project on the same material, and it is publishable: a documented concordance for a body of names is a contribution other people can cite.

What you should now be able to do
  • Open a file whose encoding you do not know, and diagnose it when the text arrives wrong.
  • Count variants and tell apart the four kinds: typographic, script, romanisation, and different-name.
  • Normalise into a new column, choosing how far to go and saying why.
  • Write a concordance that records the basis of each equivalence, so that a reader can argue with one line.
