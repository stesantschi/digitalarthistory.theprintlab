Module 04

Spatial research design

Putting prints on a map: what a point claims, and what the pattern is about.

  • ~75 min
  • requires modules 1 and 2
  • place-name lists, terrain models, Smapshot
  • CC BY 4.0

Outline. The worked example and its code are being prepared; this page gives the question, the steps and their limits.

The research question

Starting point

I have fifty prints of famous places. If I put them on a map, what will I see?

You will see a pattern, because any set of images on a map produces one, and it will look like a finding. This module helps you work out what the pattern is about. Often it describes publishing and collecting more than it describes the landscape.

The Japanese material

A meisho is a famous place: a site with an established literary and pictorial reputation, depicted according to conventions built up over centuries (Yonemoto 2003).

A designer working on a meisho series placed his print within that tradition of earlier images, poems and associations. A print of a place therefore shows that the place had standing in the tradition. Whether the designer went there is a separate question.

The computational operation

Plotting turns a statement about a place into a statement about a position. A point on a map has an exact location and no width. The format cannot express "somewhere along this stretch of river" or "a view understood to be from here".

The uncertainty of the evidence is dropped at that moment, without notice, and the map looks more precise than the evidence behind it.

Where this comes from

This module draws on Drawing from the Crowd, a platform on which volunteers georeference ukiyo-e prints against 3D terrain (Drawing from the Crowd). It is built on Smapshot (Smapshot).

The project contributes the distinction the second half of this module rests on: naming the place a print shows, and working out where it was seen from, are two different tasks that answer different questions and fail in different ways.

What we will do

Take the place names from the print titles, match them to coordinates, and look at what fails. Plot the result and read where the prints cluster. Then test the pattern against its most likely cause: the shape of the collection.

The last step asks a different question: where was the viewer standing? That needs a different method, and its failures tell you the most.

Step 1 — From title to place

Print titles contain place names, written for readers. List the place each title names, one row per print.

Choice point

How do you get the place names out?

By hand, from the series list
Slow and accurate. Fifty titles take an afternoon. At this scale it is usually the right choice.
By matching patterns in the title text
Fast, and it works only where titles follow one convention. You will need to check every result by hand anyway.
With automatic name recognition
Such software is trained on modern text. It handles historical place names unevenly and cannot know that a district no longer exists.

At fifty prints, doing it by hand is faster than correcting an automatic method. At five thousand, the balance changes.

Step 2 — Match places to coordinates

Look up each place in a gazetteer, a list of place names with coordinates, and note which list you used. Keep a separate list of the names that found no match, and read it before fixing anything.

The computational operation

Finding coordinates for a name (geocoding) is a lookup in a list someone compiled. A coordinate therefore cites that list.

When a service does not recognise a name, it often returns its best guess, formatted exactly like a confident answer. The gazetteer is a source and belongs in your bibliography.

How to read this

Names fail to match for four different reasons:

  • The name changed. The place exists under another name.
  • The place is an area. A stretch of riverbank, a road, a view across a bay.
  • The place is literary. Some titles name an imagined site, or one borrowed from a Chinese model.
  • The ground changed. Coastlines and rivers move. A site on the water then may be inland now, and a modern coordinate puts it in the wrong relation to its surroundings.

The fourth gives no warning. Everything plots, and the map is wrong.

The Japanese material

Many Edo place names changed when Edo became Tokyo in 1868, and Tokyo's wards were merged and renamed again in 1947 (source to add).

The mouth of the Sumida and Edo's bay shore have been filled in since the seventeenth century (source to add). Sites shown at the water's edge now lie inland. Modern coordinates place them correctly on today's map and wrongly in relation to the water the print is about.

Step 3 — Plot the places and read the clusters

Put the matched places on a map and note where they cluster.

How to read this

Before reading the clusters as facts about the place, check three other causes.

The series. A publisher planned a set of views as a whole, and chose which places to include.

The collection. Your prints come from institutions that collected unevenly, and their gaps become yours.

The gazetteer. Places it could not match are missing from the map, and they go missing in a pattern.

What remains after these three is a pattern about how places were depicted. Often something remains, and that is the finding.

Step 4 — Test the pattern

Count how many of your prints fall in each area. Then count the same for everything the holding institutions have from the same series. If the two counts track each other, your map shows the collection. Where they differ, you have something to explain, and that difference is the finding.

Question

Your map shows a dense cluster along the Sumida River. Is that a fact about Edo, about the series, or about your data?

Show the answer

The map alone cannot decide. The river was a centre of the city's life and a subject with long pictorial standing, so the density is plausible. It is also where a hundred-view series would concentrate (Smith and Poster 1986), and where surviving impressions are commonest.

The comparison in step 4 separates the collection from the other two causes. Separating the series' conventions from the city's geography needs the art-historical literature.

Step 5 — Locate the viewpoint as well as the place

The steps so far answer where is this a picture of. A second question is where was this seen from. A place name gives a subject; a viewpoint gives a position, a direction, a height and an angle of view.

A person marks the same features, such as a peak, a bridge or a shrine, both in the print and on a 3D terrain model. Software then calculates the position and direction from which the scene would look like the print. This is called monoplotting.

The computational operation

The calculation solves for a camera: three numbers for position, three for orientation, and the field of view (Hartley and Zisserman 2004).

It assumes the image follows one consistent perspective. When it does not, the software still returns the position that fits best, together with how far the marked points miss (the residual). The residual is the number to report.

The Japanese material

Edo prints often do not follow one consistent perspective. Viewpoints are composed: raised where no vantage point existed, compressed to fit the format, built from earlier images and guidebooks. A meisho view can combine features that cannot be seen together.

A large residual can therefore be a result. It can measure how far the image departs from any position a person could occupy, which is an art-historical finding.

How to read this

Locating the viewpoint gives three things a place name cannot.

Direction. Two prints of one site, seen from opposite sides, are one point on the map and two different pictures. The direction separates them.

Height. Many views are taken from above any ground a person could stand on. The calculation says how far above.

Fit. How badly the print resists a single viewpoint. Sorted, the residuals give a rough index of how composed each view is. Read them alongside what you already know about each print.

Choice point

Which spatial claim are you making? Both are legitimate, and they answer different questions.

The place shown
Where the print is of. Quick, works at scale from a title and a gazetteer, and says nothing about how the site was seen.
The viewpoint
Where the print was seen from. Slow, needs a person for each print, and gives direction, height and a measure of its own fit.
Both, recorded separately
The right choice for a set of prints you want to argue from, and the reason a project like this works with volunteers.

Merging the two into one coordinate column is the commonest way a spatial dataset loses its value for the question it was built for.

What this cannot tell you

  • Whether the artist went there. Many meisho views were made from earlier images, guidebooks and convention. A depiction records a tradition as well as a place.
  • Where the viewer stood, except under a condition. Step 5 returns the position that best fits the print, which is meaningful only if the print follows one perspective. Otherwise, report the residual.
  • An area as an area. Everything plotted becomes a point, which looks more precise than the evidence.
  • The historical ground. Modern coordinates on a modern map describe today's country. Where coast, rivers or streets have changed, the map is accurate and misleading at once.
  • Places never depicted. Gaps on the map describe the pictorial tradition and the collection, and they look like facts about the place.

Use your own materials

  1. Start from the cleaned list from module 1, with names already checked against your concordance.
  2. Add your own gazetteer rows where the list lacks a place, with the source of each coordinate in its own column.
  3. Do step 4 against the full holdings of the institutions you used. It is the step most often skipped, and the one that keeps the argument sound.
  4. Locate the viewpoint for ten of your prints by hand before deciding whether step 5 suits your scale.

Before you plot, write down the pattern you expect and what would count against it. A map readily confirms whatever you bring to it.

What you should now be able to do
  • Take place names from titles and say what was lost on the way.
  • Sort failed matches into four kinds: changed name, area, literary place, changed ground.
  • Explain why a point on a map claims more precision than the evidence has.
  • Test a spatial pattern against the collection that produced it.
  • Tell the place shown from the viewpoint, and say which one your dataset records.
  • Read a residual as a measure of how far a print departs from any position a person could occupy.
