Module 02

Japanese images and metadata in art-historical research

Reading what a record actually contains — and what it doesn't.

  • ~75 min
  • requires module 1
  • MET, NDL, Japan Search, ARC
  • CC BY 4.0

Outline. The worked example and its code are being prepared; this page gives the question, the steps and their limits.

The research question

Starting point

I have an image of an Edo-period print and I want to find out who made it. Where do I look, and what do the different answers mean?

The credit block above is the exercise, run once by hand before the code runs it three hundred times. Nine fields, and they are not nine of the same thing.

The opening number is printed on the sheet, in the gutter: 下ノ二. The holding institution and the licence are facts about the object's present situation, checkable by anyone. The designer and the date are conclusions, arrived at by argument from evidence this record does not show you — and they arrive in the same typeface as the rest.

The capture date is there because everything above it can change. A licence is revised, an institution transfers a holding, an attribution is revisited. Without a date, the record claims a currency it cannot have.

A natural question, and one that turns into a metadata problem the moment you try to answer it. Four major databases hold records that might match your print. They use different fields, different attribution conventions, different romanisation, and different rules about what counts as "the maker". Before you can compare them, you have to read each one on its own terms.

The Japanese material

An Edo print designer worked under an art name, a gō, and changed it — sometimes several times across a career, sometimes to mark a new stage of work, sometimes on inheriting a studio.

The man catalogued as Hokusai signed as Shunrō, Sōri, Taito, Iitsu and Gakyō Rōjin Manji at different points. All are him. None is a mistake.

So a search on one name returns part of a body of work, and a record giving a different name is not necessarily describing a different person.

The computational operation

A query is a claim about what belongs together. Searching on a name asserts that the name is the thing shared by the records you want, and the database applies that assertion literally: Hokusai, hokusai and 北斎 are three unrelated strings to it.

So the recall of your search is bounded by the reach of your query, and the gap between them is invisible in the results. You see what matched, never what did not.

Choice point

Before any database: which question are you actually asking? These three look like the same one.

Who made this print?
Returns the designer, because that is the field the databases fill. The block cutter, the printer and the publisher disappear, and nothing in the result records that they were dropped.
Which name does each institution give as maker?
Returns four answers and makes the divergence visible. Still silent about why each institution chose as it did.
Which institutions name a maker, on what basis, and where do they disagree?
Names its own evidence and its own failure condition. It can come back empty, which is what makes it worth asking.

This module asks the third. The starting point above is how the question arrives; this is what it has to become before a database can be useful.

What we will do

Pull records for the same print from four databases. Compare what each one tells us. Identify where they agree, where they disagree, and what the disagreement is about.

The four are chosen because they answer to different traditions. The Metropolitan Museum describes an object it owns. The National Diet Library describes a publication. Japan Search aggregates across institutions and inherits whatever each contributed. The Ritsumeikan Art Research Center describes prints as a specialist collection built by researchers. None of these is the neutral one.

Step 1 — Query each database

Start with the Met, whose open-access data comes as a plain table. Reading it needs one library and three lines.

The computational operation

The pattern here — open, filter, select — recurs in every module, and each of the three steps is a decision about evidence.

Filtering is corpus construction: the rows you keep become your material, and the rows you drop leave no trace in anything downstream. Selecting columns decides which properties of the object are in play.

Three fields, three different kinds of claim. The name is an attribution. The dates after it are biographical fact about a person. ca. 1830–32 is a dating of the object, and the ca. is the museum telling you it is an estimate.

Choice point

Which field do you search on? The answer shapes everything downstream.

Artist Display Name
Human-readable, and what you would say aloud. It varies by institution and by romanisation, so it travels badly between databases.
Accession or call number
Exact and reliable, and meaningful in one institution only. Useless for comparison.
An authority identifier, where one is given
Travels between institutions and is missing from most records for Japanese material. This is the gap module 5 addresses.

This module searches on names, and part of what it demonstrates is the cost of doing so.

Step 2 — Read the same print elsewhere

The National Diet Library and Japan Search return their answers over the network rather than as a file. The shape of the code changes; the reasoning does not.

The Japanese material

Library and museum cataloguing in Japan developed along separate lines and answer separate questions.

A library describes a publication: edition, series, extent, publisher. A museum describes an object: this impression, its condition, its provenance.

The same print held in both gets two records that are each accurate and mutually incomplete. Neither is failing at the other's job.

The computational operation

USE_LIVE decides what your analysis is of. Left at False, it reads a copy captured on a known date, so the result is reproducible and datable. Set to True, it queries the institution now, so the result reflects today's record and cannot be re-run identically.

Both are legitimate. An argument about what a catalogue says should cite a captured state; an argument about current practice should be live. Say which you did.

The field names differ from the Met's, and the absences matter as much as the values. A library record carrying no creator is not an oversight: it reflects a cataloguing rule about what a publication's responsible party is, applied to a form of publication where the designer, the block cutter, the printer and the publisher all have a claim.

The Japanese material

A woodblock print is made by at least four people: the designer who supplies the drawing, the block cutter, the printer, and the publisher who commissions and finances it.

Western cataloguing conventions place the designer in the artist field and the rest in a note, or nowhere. That choice is an argument about authorship, made once and then invisible.

Step 3 — Put the four side by side

How to read this

The source_of_date column is empty in every row, and that is the finding. Each institution states a date. None states where the date came from.

Agreement between rows is weak evidence when the rows are not independent. Aggregators inherit their fields from contributors, and contributors copy from published catalogues, so four records saying the same thing may be one record said four times.

Disagreement is the more useful signal. It tells you a decision was made, which means there is something to find.

Question

Three of the four give the same date. Does that make it more likely to be right?

Show the answer

Only if the three arrived at it independently, and usually they did not. Trace each back and you often reach one printed catalogue, from which everything else descends.

Trace each back far enough and you often reach one printed catalogue, from which everything else descends. A date recorded in a dozen places, resting on a single mid-century source, with no corroboration on any surviving sheet, is a common enough pattern to be worth expecting rather than discovering.

A worked case: the Great Picture Book of Everything

Everything above is easier to see on a body of material where the record demonstrably changed, in public, recently, and is documented at every stage. The 103 Hokusai drawings the British Museum acquired in 2020 are that case.

They were made for an illustrated encyclopaedia, Banmotsu ehon daizen zu, which was never published. Block-ready drawings, roughly postcard-sized, brushed on thin paper and pasted onto card, kept in a wooden box inscribed with the title and Hokusai's name. Two of the sheets carry the seal of Henri Vever, the French Art Nouveau jeweller and collector. They were sold at a Paris auction in 1948, entered a private French collection, and were not publicly recorded again for seventy years.

Then, at a Paris sale in June 2019, they appeared catalogued as the work of Katsushika Issai, a pupil. The dealer Israel Goldman bought them for €110,000 on the conviction that they were by Hokusai himself; the British Museum's Timothy Clark agreed, and the Museum acquired them the following year.

How to read this

Run the four checks against this one body of work and each returns something.

Where did the field come from? The maker field said Issai in June 2019 and Hokusai by September 2020. Both were records; one was an auction catalogue and one a museum acquisition. The object did not change.

Does the object carry it? Partly. The box carries the title and Hokusai's name, and the box was made perhaps seventy years after the drawings. Two sheets carry a collector's seal. Nothing carries a date.

Who else says it, independently? Before 2019, nobody said anything: the drawings were absent from every record for seventy years. Absence in the catalogues was not evidence about the drawings.

Has anyone dissented? Here the interesting movement is within one institution rather than between two. The Museum's 2020 announcement gives the drawings as created in 1829. Its exhibition page gives them as produced in the 1820s–1840s. That is a twenty-year widening, published by the same institution, as research proceeded.

Question

Your dataset was built in 2020 and records the date as 1829. Is it wrong?

Show the answer

It is accurate as to what the source said when you captured it, and out of step with what the same source says now. Those are different failures and only one of them is yours.

A record of the capture date turns the first into a citation and the second into a known limitation. Without it, your dataset silently contradicts the institution it drew from, and a later reader has no way to tell whether you disagreed or simply arrived early.

This is why the credit block at the top of this page carries a captured field, and why the register in data/ has one for every file.

All 103 drawings are on the Museum's Collection online, and they also sit in Late Hokusai, a research instance holding the artist's late work across the British Museum, the Metropolitan Museum and the Freer|Sackler. That is this module's four-database comparison already built, by people who had the same problem.

Sources: British Museum acquisition announcement (2020); Hokusai: The Great Picture Book of Everything, exhibition and catalogue (2021); trade press reporting of the 2019 Piasa sale.

What this cannot tell you

The comparison shows you where records diverge. It does not tell you which is correct, and nothing in this module can.

  • It cannot date the object. Only the sheet can do that, through a colophon, a censor's seal, or a publisher's address in a datable form.
  • It cannot establish independence. Whether two records descend from one source is a bibliographic question, answered in the literature rather than in the data.
  • It cannot see what was never catalogued. Absence in four databases is evidence about four databases.
  • It cannot resolve names by itself. Matching 北斎 to Hokusai to Hokousaï takes either an authority file or your own judgement. Module 1 covers the mechanics; the judgement stays yours.

What it does give you is a shortlist of places where the record was decided rather than observed. That shortlist is where the art-historical work starts.

Use your own materials

Every step above works on a print of your choosing.

  1. Replace the accession number in step 1 with one from a collection you work on.
  2. Set USE_LIVE = True and put your own search terms in step 2.
  3. Run step 3 unchanged. The comparison table takes whatever rows you give it.

Then write four sentences: which fields diverge, what each institution appears to be describing, which claim you trust least, and what evidence would settle it. That last sentence is the one worth bringing to a supervisor.

What you should now be able to do
  • Pull a record from a museum table, a library service and an aggregator, and read each on its own terms.
  • Say what kind of claim each field carries: observation, inference, or convention.
  • Recognise that agreement between records is weak evidence when the records are not independent.
  • Name what a metadata comparison cannot settle, and what would settle it.
