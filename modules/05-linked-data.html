Module 05

Linked data

Connecting your research to other people's, so that both can be reused.

  • ~110 min
  • requires modules 1–4
  • Japan Search, NDL Authorities, Wikidata, ResearchSpace
  • CC BY 4.0

Outline. The worked example and its code are being prepared; this page gives the question, the steps and their limits.

The research question

Starting point

I've built a dataset of two hundred prints. How do I publish it so that someone else's project can use it, and mine can use theirs?

The obstacle is that your dataset refers to things by writing their names, and so does everyone else's, and no two people write them the same way. Module 1 solved that inside one project with a concordance. This module is the same problem between projects, where you cannot ask everyone to adopt your spellings.

The computational operation

Linked data replaces names with identifiers: instead of the string Katsushika Hokusai, an address that stands for the person.

Everything is then written as three-part statements — subject, property, object — of the form this print, was designed by, that person. A pile of such statements from many sources joins up automatically, because the identifiers match even when the labels do not.

The Japanese material

Coverage of Japanese material in the international authority files is thin and uneven. Major designers have entries; block cutters, printers, minor publishers and most individual works do not.

Japanese infrastructures fill part of the gap — the ARC databases, Japan Search, the National Diet Library's authorities — and they were built to different models.

So reconciliation for this material is partial by nature, and a project that reports only its matches reports the wrong half.

What we will do

Give the things in your dataset identifiers of their own. Reconcile what can be reconciled against existing authorities, and count what cannot. Express the relationships as statements. Then face the harder problem: saying, in a form a machine will carry, where a claim came from and who disputes it.

Step 1 — Give your things identifiers

Two properties make an identifier useful: it never changes, and it belongs to somebody who intends to keep it resolving. Both are commitments about the future rather than technical features, which is why the domain you publish under matters more than the format you publish in.

Step 2 — Query Japanese institutional RDF

Two Japanese institutions publish their holdings as RDF and answer queries directly, which makes them the right place to see what a data model decides. Japan Search aggregates across roughly 160 contributing databases and serves the result over a SPARQL endpoint. Web NDL Authorities publishes the National Diet Library's authority file the same way, in RDF/XML, Turtle and JSON-LD.

The computational operation

SPARQL asks for a shape rather than a keyword. The WHERE block describes a pattern of triples with holes in it, marked ?item, ?who, and the store returns every set of values that fills the holes.

Which means an empty result is ambiguous in a way a search box result is not. It can mean the thing is absent, or that the shape you described is not the shape the data uses.

Then the authority file, which answers a different question: what does this institution consider one person, and what does it call them?

How to read this

The variant forms are the interesting field. An authority record exists because a name has more than one written shape, and it records which shapes the institution has decided are the same person. That is module 1's concordance, built by a national library, published as data.

The outward links matter too. Web NDL Authorities has added links to Wikidata, so a record can carry both a Japanese institutional identifier and an international one. Where both are present, a name travels. Where only one is, it stops at a border.

Choice point

Which authority do you reconcile against? Each answers to a different tradition and covers a different part of the field.

Web NDL Authorities
Built for Japanese material, with variant forms and readings recorded properly. Organised around library cataloguing, so it is strong on people who published and thinner on people who cut blocks.
Japan Search
Reach across museums, libraries, archives and universities at once. Its values are normalised on the way in, which is convenient and is also a decision made on your behalf. See step 4.
ARC, Ritsumeikan
Built by print researchers, so it records publisher marks, seals and states that general systems have no field for. Less widely linked outside Japan.
Wikidata
The widest reach, openly editable, and the hub most other datasets point at. Coverage of this material thins quickly past the major names, and what you may add to it is governed by rules worth reading before you start. See below.
VIAF, ULAN
Library and museum authorities with editorial review behind them. Narrower, slower to change, organised around Western conventions of authorship.

Reconcile against more than one and record each result separately, including the misses. A reconciliation rate is a finding about the authority file's coverage of your material, and for this material it is often the most interesting number in the project.

Wikidata is the one most people reach for, and the one where the temptation is strongest: you find a gap, and the platform invites you to fill it. It is worth knowing the rules before you spend an afternoon that gets reverted.

Caution — what Wikidata will accept

An item has to meet at least one of three conditions. It carries a valid sitelink to a Wikimedia project page; or it refers to a clearly identifiable entity describable using serious and publicly available references; or it fulfils a structural need, meaning it is required to make statements on another item more useful.

Read against this material, that means: major designers are safe on the first condition. A block cutter, a printer, a minor publisher or an individual print usually is not, and has to earn its place on the second or third. In practice the second is satisfied by external identifiers — an ARC record, an NDL authority, a published catalogue — and an item resting on none of those is the kind that gets listed for deletion.

The rules are also in motion. A reform discussion running through 2026 has raised whether items without external identifiers should face deletion, whether there should be a grace period, and — directly relevant here — how not to deter researchers and institutions who arrive wanting to share open data. Check the current policy rather than trusting a description of it, including this one.

Where your data falls outside the bar, the project's own answer is Wikibase: the same software, run as your own instance, with no notability rule but also no community and no incoming links. That is a real trade, and it is the trade this whole module is about.

A case for the notability rule

Test the three conditions against something real. The British Museum holds 103 Hokusai drawings made for an encyclopaedia that was never published, acquired in 2020 and all of them online.

The set passes easily. It has an exhibition, a catalogue, press coverage and a Wikipedia article to point at.

Each individual drawing is the hard case, and there are 103 of them. No sitelink. Their claim under the second condition rests on the Museum's catalogue and the published volume, both of which are serious and public — so the drawings clear the bar, and clear it because a curator wrote a book about them. Change the example to 103 sheets in a regional collection with no monograph and the same three conditions return a different answer.

The block cutter who would have cut them never existed, because the book was never published. There is no name, no record, and nothing for a data model to attach a role to. Absence here is a fact about the object's history rather than a gap to fill.

Notability is not a measure of importance. It is a measure of how much has already been written down, which correlates with importance unevenly and with the geography of scholarship rather closely.

The provenance a claim should carry

The same drawings show what step 4 is asking for, because their record has a documented hole in it.

They were sold in Paris in 1948, passed into a private French collection, and were not publicly recorded for seventy years. In June 2019 they surfaced at a Paris sale catalogued as the work of a pupil, Katsushika Issai; a dealer bought them convinced they were Hokusai's, a curator agreed, and the Museum acquired them in 2020.

So the maker field has a date on which it changed, an agent who changed it, and a basis. The dating has moved too, from 1829 in the acquisition announcement to the 1820s–1840s on the exhibition page — the same institution, revising in public as the research proceeded.

Nearly none of that survives into a linked data record built on schema.org. creator takes one value with no date and no author. The seventy-year gap, which is among the most informative things about these sheets, has no field at all.

This is the case to hold in mind when reading how ResearchSpace models belief and inference below. It was built at the institution that now holds these drawings, for exactly this kind of problem.

Step 3 — Write the statements

The Japanese material

creator is a single-valued property, and a print has four hands behind it: designer, block cutter, printer, publisher.

Putting the designer there and the rest in a note is the cataloguing convention module 2 examined, now hardened into a data model. Vocabularies that distinguish roles — CIDOC-CRM among them — cost more effort and let you say what actually happened.

Choice point

Which vocabulary? Each is an argument about what is worth saying.

schema.org
What Japan Search builds on. Widely understood, indexed by search engines, shaped for describing things on the web rather than for arguing about them.
CIDOC-CRM
Built by and for museums, organised around events rather than objects — a production, with participants in distinct roles, at a time, in a place. Several more statements per claim, and it can say what actually happened.
CIDOC-CRM with CRMinf
The extension for argumentation: belief, inference, premise, and who holds what on what grounds. The only common vocabulary that treats an assertion as a thing with an author. See step 4.
IIIF plus your own fields
Sufficient where the object is the point and the argument lives in prose. Honest, and it does not travel.

Step 4 — Publish the uncertainty with the claim

Here is where the arc of these five modules closes. Everything above assumes your dataset states facts. Most of what you have is claims with sources, and some of them are disputed.

Start with what Japan Search does before you see anything. Its documentation is explicit: during conversion into its RDF schema, the values for time, place and person or organisation are normalised as far as possible, given URIs, and linked outward to the hubs that other datasets point at. That is what makes the aggregate searchable, and it is a large interpretive act performed at scale.

The Japanese material

Normalising "when" for early modern Japan means resolving era names and reign years onto a single calendar. Normalising "who" means deciding that two written forms of a name are one person.

Both are the questions modules 1 and 2 spent their length on. Here they arrive already answered, by an institution, in a form that carries no trace of the answering.

The normalised value and the value the contributing institution supplied are both in the graph, and a query that asks only for the normalised one will never know it made a choice. Asking for both is one extra line and a different kind of result.

How to read this

The example is constructed. The pattern is not.

Those last three keys are invented, and no widely used vocabulary supplies them straightforwardly. Expressing "this date was asserted by one printed catalogue, rests on an inference rather than a colophon, and has been contested in print" takes deliberate modelling work in every schema in common use. CIDOC-CRM can carry it, at the cost of several more statements per claim; schema.org, which Japan Search builds on, has no comfortable place for it at all.

That difficulty is not incidental. The data models the field has built make it easy to state a date and hard to state where it came from, and a claim published without its provenance is indistinguishable downstream from an observation.

Which is how a date resting on one mid-century catalogue comes to appear, unqualified, in a dozen records.

Where this has been worked out

The problem above is not new, and one platform has taken it as its central design question. ResearchSpace, built at the British Museum and released as open source, treats a research assertion as a thing in its own right: something asserted by someone, on a stated basis, which others can strengthen or weaken.

There is a running instance for exactly the material in this module. Late Hokusai holds the artist's late work, including the 103 rediscovered drawings, drawing together holdings from the British Museum, the Metropolitan Museum and the Freer|Sackler. It is the cross-institutional comparison of module 2, built as a graph rather than assembled by hand each time.

The project ran from 2016 to 2019 and the instance carries a build date in 2021 — which is to say it is a published version rather than a maintained service, finished in the year its own subject matter changed. That is the honourable way to age, and it is the model this practice book follows.

Its model distinguishes the grounds. An assertion can rest on direct observation, on adopting somebody else's belief, or on inference from premises that are themselves resources in the system — data, images, narratives, other arguments. Each carries its provenance with it. The underlying vocabulary is CIDOC-CRM with the CRMinf extension, which supplies the belief and inference classes that schema.org has no equivalent for.

The move worth taking from it, whatever you build on: reasoning is modelled as strengthening or weakening a hypothesis, rather than as proving or disproving a fact. That is much closer to how the argument actually runs in an art-historical paper, and it is why the improvised keys above feel improvised.

Worth reading before you design your own fields. Worth adopting only if the argument is the point of your project, since the cost is a platform rather than a file.

Question

Open a drawing in the Late Hokusai instance and note its URI. Does that address return data to a machine, or only a page to a person?

Show the answer

Find out rather than assume — this is a two-minute check and the answer decides whether the resource is linkable or only visitable.

Request the URI asking for RDF instead of HTML. In a browser, a client that lets you set an Accept header will do; from a notebook, requests.get(uri, headers={"Accept": "text/turtle"}). Compare what comes back with what the browser shows you.

Interfaces built as client-side applications sometimes render everything in the browser, in which case a machine following the link receives an empty shell. Where that happens the graph is real and the addresses are not usable as linked data, which is a common and easily missed gap between a knowledge graph and a published one.

Whatever you find, record it with the date. That is the same discipline as the capture date in module 2, applied to an infrastructure rather than a record.

Question

Your dataset carries a date you believe is wrong. Do you publish it?

Show the answer

Yes, with its source and your dissent attached. Omitting it makes your dataset disagree silently with every other, and silent disagreement is the condition this whole practice book is trying to end.

Publishing the claim, its origin, and the objection lets a later reader trace the disagreement instead of inheriting one side of it.

Step 6 — Contribute what is missing

Everything so far has been reading. This step is writing, into a resource other people depend on, and it is a different act with different obligations.

Reconciling against an authority costs nothing and risks nothing. Creating an entry in one puts a claim into circulation under your name, in a place where it will be copied, cached, and eventually treated as a source by people who will never see how it got there. Modules 1 to 5 have been an argument about what that does. This is the step where you do it.

The computational operation

Creating an item mints a new identifier and makes it immediately resolvable. Nothing validates it beyond community review after the fact.

The identifier is permanent in the sense that it keeps resolving. What it means is not: items are merged, split, revised and deleted, and a merge leaves a redirect pointing at something that may no longer be quite what you cited.

The Japanese material

The gaps you will want to fill are the ones the field has been thinnest on: block cutters, printers, minor publishers, the poets in a kyōka anthology, individual sheets.

These are exactly the entries most likely to be challenged, because the evidence for them sits in Japanese-language scholarship and in specialist databases that a reviewer elsewhere may not recognise as serious references.

An ARC or NDL identifier attached to the item is the single most effective thing you can do about that.

Choice point

Where does a new entry go? The four answers differ in what they cost and what they promise.

Wikidata
Free, immediately linked, immediately reused. Governed by the notability rule, editable by anyone, and out of your control from the moment you save. The right home for entities that clear the bar and that you want other projects to find.
Your own Wikibase
No notability rule, full control, your own vocabulary. No community, no incoming links, and one more thing you have to keep running. The right home for material that will not be accepted elsewhere and that you are committed to maintaining.
A domain authority
Proposing an addition to ARC, NDL or a subject register is slower and produces an entry with review behind it. The right route where the entity belongs to a field that already has a keeper.
Your own dataset, published and cited
The lowest-cost option and the most frequently correct one. A documented concordance with a DOI is a contribution, and other people can reconcile against it without anybody needing permission.

The fourth is undersold. Not every finding needs to become an identifier in a global graph to be reusable.

Before you create anything

Search properly first. The entity may exist under a romanisation you did not try, or in Japanese script only. Duplicates are worse than absences, because they split the links that were meant to converge.

Bring a reference to every statement. An item with no external identifiers and no cited source is the kind that gets listed for deletion, and rightly.

Say what is uncertain. Both a rank and a qualifier exist for this. Use them rather than asserting a contested date flatly, because flat assertion is the thing this whole practice book has been arguing against.

Record what you did. Which items you created, on what date, from which sources. This is your own contribution to a shared resource, and it belongs in your project's documentation as much as your data does.

Expect to be edited. Someone will change what you wrote. If the change is wrong, the remedy is a conversation with sources, not a revert.

Question

You have identified a block cutter named in a colophon, absent from every authority file. Should you create the item?

Show the answer

Ask what it would be for. If the name will carry statements on works that are themselves described — this person cut these blocks for that book — the structural need condition is met and the item does real work.

If it is one name from one colophon with nothing yet attached, publishing it in your own dataset with the colophon cited is more useful and more honest. It stays reconcilable, it carries its evidence, and it does not stake a claim in a shared graph on a single sheet of evidence.

The question is not whether the person deserves an entry. It is whether an entry is the right form for what you currently know.

What this cannot tell you

  • It cannot make disagreement go away. Two reconciled datasets that disagree now disagree in a form a machine can detect, which is progress and not resolution.
  • It cannot supply coverage that does not exist. Where the authorities are thin for Japanese material, linking is thin, and no technique fixes that from your desk. On Wikidata you may not be permitted to fix it even where you could.
  • It cannot guarantee that what you cited stays put. An openly edited item can be merged, revised or deleted by someone else. The identifier survives a merge as a redirect; the meaning may not. Record the date you reconciled, alongside the identifier.
  • It cannot show you what normalisation removed. An aggregator's normalised value and its source value are both in the graph; a query that asks for one will never report that it chose.
  • It cannot express everything you know. Every vocabulary is a set of decisions about what is worth saying, made by people with their own material in view.
  • It cannot guarantee anyone reuses it. Publishing well raises the odds and promises nothing.
  • It cannot outlive your commitment to the identifiers. A URI is a promise to keep something resolving, and institutions break that promise. The British Museum's own semantic web service — the one behind much early CIDOC-CRM work — went down repeatedly and its URIs eventually returned 404, taking with them every external link that had been built on them. An institutional domain is not a guarantee. Choose a domain you will still control in ten years, and expect the ones you link out to not to be there.

Use your own materials

  1. Mint identifiers under a domain you own, not an institutional one you may lose.
  2. Reconcile against at least two authorities, one Japanese and one international, and record both results including the misses.
  3. For every date and attribution, add a field saying who asserted it and where. Even as free text, even in a spreadsheet, this is the highest-value column in the file.
  4. Publish the data, the concordance from module 1, and a note on what the collection excludes.
  5. Before designing your own provenance fields, read how CRMinf models belief and inference. Borrowing its distinctions costs nothing; building its platform is a separate decision.
What you should now be able to do
  • Mint stable identifiers and say what commitment they represent.
  • Reconcile against an authority file and report the failures as a finding.
  • Write your data as statements, and recognise where the vocabulary is making an argument for you.
  • Attach provenance to a claim, and say why most schemas make that harder than it should be.
  • Decide where a new entry belongs, and what creating one in a shared resource obliges you to.
  • Name a vocabulary that does model assertion, belief and inference, and say what adopting it would cost.
