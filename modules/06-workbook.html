Workbook: displaying the illustrated book

The step-by-step process behind module 6, from finding a book online to presenting a digital display of it. Print it, or fill it in as you go.

How to use this workbook

  1. Choose one early modern Japanese illustrated book

    Ideally one you work with. For the records and the viewer tests you need a digitised copy in a repository that provides a IIIF Manifest. For the publishing tools you can also use your own photographs.

  2. Find it in two repositories, three if you can

    Where to look lists the repositories. If your book is held in only one place, pick one from Books to practise on.

  3. Fill in one record for each repository

    Save the Manifest address whenever you find one. You need it to test the viewers.

  4. Open the book in each repository's own viewer

    Note what it does well and where it goes wrong, under Judge each repository.

  5. Test other viewers, write captions, build the display

    In a class, everything up to here is the preparation, and the rest is done together.

When you get stuck

Note where. The same book is often catalogued under different titles in different places, so a title search may miss it. Write down the search terms you used and what happened. A record of what failed is evidence, and as useful as what worked.

What you will make: a published digital display of your book, and a three-minute presentation of it that says why you chose your viewer and what it allowed you to do.

What a IIIF Manifest is

Imagine a shoebox of loose photographs with a slip of paper on top: wedding, 1962, in order. When a library digitises the box, it makes two things. One is the images. The other is the slip: a note saying which images belong together and in what order.

The digital slip is called a Manifest. Libraries agreed on a shared format for it, called IIIF (pronounced “triple-eye-eff”), so that any display software, called a viewer, can show any library's images.

To work with a book, you only need the Manifest's address. It usually ends in manifest.json. Copy it and paste it into a viewer, and the book appears.

Where to find the address

  1. The IIIF logo on the object page, a small square of coloured bars. Right-click it and choose “Copy link address”.
  2. A text link near the image reading “IIIF Manifest” or “IIIF”. Right-click and copy.
  3. Inside a viewer already running on the page. In Mirador, open the information panel and scroll to the bottom. In the IIIF Curation Viewer, the information button is top right.

Practise on a playbill printed in Osaka in 1849, which reads right to left. The Manifest is code; the recipe page opens it in five viewers (scroll to Example 1).

If a repository shows no Manifest, the IIIF guide to finding resources explains, institution by institution, where to look. The address must begin with https. If one viewer shows nothing, try a second before deciding the address is wrong.

After you have seen the viewers: five questions

A catalogue record informs a person, who can doubt it and check it. A Manifest instructs a program, which carries it out whether or not it suits the book. Assessing a viewer is therefore a form of catalogue criticism. Ask:

QuestionManifest field (version 3)What to watch
Which way does it read?viewingDirectionSomeone has to state it. Without it, viewers default to left to right.
Is the unit the page or the opening?behaviorThese books were designed as openings. Shown as single pages, every picture across the gutter is cut in half. Scrolls and accordion bindings need their own settings.
Who says so, and on what ground?metadataArtist, date and format travel with the image and look settled. Check them against the book itself, as you would secondary literature.
What is here, and what is missing?itemsA library holding volumes 2 and 5 of fifteen may list just those two, and a viewer may join them as if they were consecutive.
What may be reproduced?rightsCheck the rights before publishing beyond the classroom.

Older Manifests and some viewers use IIIF version 2, where items is called sequences and behavior is viewingHint. The questions stay the same.

Remember

When a viewer shows your book wrongly, the cause can be the Manifest, the viewer, or both, and each needs a different fix. Start by documenting it: which viewer did what, on which book.

Record what each repository says

Fill in one copy for each repository that holds your book.

QuestionRepository 1Repository 2
Which repository is this?Name, and link to the record page
What title does the record give?Copy it exactly, with spelling and macrons
What title is printed on the book?On the cover slip (daisen) or the first page
Does the record list other titles?Variant titles, other romanisations, series titles
What date does it give, and where does the date come from?Colophon (okuzuke), preface, cataloguer's estimate, or not stated
Who is named as the artist?As the record names them, and any signature in the book
How many volumes, and which are held here?For example: 3 vols., vols. 1 and 3 held
What is the Manifest address?Full link, starting https. Otherwise “none found”
How good are the images, and what may I do with them?Can you zoom to individual lines? Reuse: CC0, research only, no statement?
Where did I get stuck?Exact search terms, and what happened
Where do the record and the book disagree?A date, a title, a missing volume, pages out of order

Viewers

Theseus
A IIIF viewer. Paste the Manifest address after https://theseusviewer.org/?iiif-content=, or use “Quick open”.
IIIF Curation Viewer (CODH)
Built in Japan for Japanese material, with a Japanese interface. Reads version 2 Manifests. Paste the address after https://codh.rois.ac.jp/software/iiif-curation-viewer/demo/?manifest=.

Publishing tools

Storiiies (free account)
Takes a Manifest, with a specific page, and returns a shareable annotated story. Add a title and an email address; the editing link arrives by email. Zoom until one detail fills the frame and click “Add annotation”. See a finished example.
Mirador (no account)
The research viewer used across libraries and museums. Open a second window and set the two to different pages or to two different copies, with deep zoom and annotation in both. It is built for comparison: what you draw is lost on reload, so export the workspace before closing the tab. One-click alternative: projectmirador.org/embed/?iiif-content= followed by the Manifest address.
StoryMapJS (Google account)
Moves a reader through points on a map, one image and a short text at each. It takes uploaded image files, so the record stays behind: reading direction, rights and the link to the library are lost. It suits books about places, or where the place of production matters. Ask first: which places does my book discuss? Tutorial.

Further IIIF tools

Universal Viewer
The embeddable viewer most museums use. Handles IIIF images, audio and video, plus 3D and PDF.
Glycerine Viewer
Drag the IIIF logo from a museum page onto it. A workbench for annotating collections together.
Digirati Manifest Editor
Create and edit IIIF Manifests visually.
All IIIF tools
The list maintained by the IIIF community.

Write the caption

Museums keep object labels to around 150 words, for visitors with little time. Write for the same reader: highly educated, most likely in something else, and new to your book. Assume intelligence and no prior knowledge. The task with an illustrated book is to connect what is visible on the page with what the object is, who made it, what it was for, and why it matters.

  1. IPut the object at the centre

    Point at what the reader can see: the line, the wear, the colour, the design running across the fold. If a sentence would still be true with a different book on the screen, it belongs elsewhere.

  2. IIName the one uncertainty that matters

    Saying what is not known is more interesting than false confidence, and it makes readers curious. State that one uncertainty plainly and write the rest directly.

  3. IIIBring in the people

    Someone cut these blocks, bought the book, bound it, retitled it, sold it on. As Julie Nelson Davis's work shows, that world matters, and the books carry small evidence of it (Ginzburg's clues). A curator can point to it.

  4. IVWrite as you would speak

    Read the caption aloud and keep what you would say to a colleague in front of the work. Use the active voice: people made things. Prefer the short word, and vary sentence length.

Further reading: Lucy Trench, Gallery Text at the V&A: A Ten Point Guide (2018).

Create and present your display

Decide what you want to say, and with which tool, before you open any app.

What my book is
Which openings I am showing, and in what order
What I want my audience to understand
What my material needs a tool to do

What goes in: a selection of openings, with your captions. Check what the accompanying record says about who supplied it, on what ground and when, and how that data relates to the object.

Where to publish: choose one of the three publishing tools. Storiiies is the default: it keeps the reading direction, the openings, the rights line and the link to the holding library. Mirador is the research route: two copies or two openings side by side, with your spoken argument in place of a caption. StoryMapJS suits books where geography carries the story.

Keep a record of what you made. For Storiiies, the short code from your share link, placed after https://manifest.storiiies-editor.cogapp.com/v3/. For Mirador, the Manifest address and the exported workspace. For StoryMapJS, the URL.

Present it in three minutes

Use the Three Minute Thesis (3MT) format, with your display on screen as the visual aid.

  1. Hook

    Open with one detail or paradox the audience can see: a stain, a worn line, a recut block.

  2. Why

    State the problem without jargon. What did the way your book was made require that the tool could not render? Say “the book reads from what a European would call the back”, not “viewingDirection”.

  3. How

    Move through your display and say why you built it that way. Let the display show the evidence while you make the argument.

  4. So what

    Say what the tool could not do and what you did about it. Then say what this means for anyone displaying a book like yours.
