Glossary

Two vocabularies, in the two directions readers arrive from. Read the part you need and skip the other.

The parts mirror the two side-panels in the modules. Terms marked with a dotted underline anywhere on this site link here. Most of these words describe something you already know under another name, and where that is true the entry says so.

One word, three meanings

Index. A printed index — a catalogue raisonné, a checklist, a standard reference — is a scholarly work with an author, a date and a method, and records descend from these. An index in a book is the everyday sense, and the analogy these modules use for a Python dictionary. An index in code is a position in a list, counted from zero.

Where this site means the first, it says "printed index". The ambiguity is worth holding on to: a date can travel from the first sense to the third without anyone deciding it should.

The Japanese material

What the sources require: names, formats, and the conventions that govern how this material was made and described.

The four hands

A woodblock print is made by at least four people: the designer who supplies the drawing, the block cutter (彫師), the printer (摺師), and the publisher (版元) who commissions and finances it.

Cataloguing convention places the designer in the artist field and the rest in a note, or nowhere. That is an argument about authorship, made once and then rendered invisible by the interface.

See also publisher.

Romanisation

Writing Japanese in the Latin alphabet. Hepburn is standard in English-language scholarship; Kunrei and older systems appear in older records, and French and German catalogues bring their own conventions.

Hokusai therefore appears as Hokusai, Hokousaï and ホクサイ in different records, and a database treats those as three unrelated things.

State, impression, edition

Three different claims that records routinely conflate.

An impression is one sheet pulled from the blocks. An edition is a printing run. A state is a stage of the blocks themselves, after recutting, replacement or the addition of a new publisher's mark.

Blocks wear across thousands of impressions, so two sheets from one set of blocks can differ visibly. Where a record says "another impression", ask which of the three it means.

Japan Search ジャパンサーチ

A national aggregator gathering records from Japanese museums, libraries, archives and universities into one search.

It inherits its fields from its contributors, so a record found there is generally the contributing institution's record, reshaped. Finding the same claim in Japan Search and at the contributing museum is one claim seen twice.

It publishes its aggregate as RDF and answers SPARQL queries. On the way in, values for time, place and person are normalised as far as possible and given URIs — which is what makes the whole searchable, and is a decision made on your behalf before you see a record. Module 3 works this directly.

Georeferencing

Assigning a position on the earth to an image. The simple form matches a place name against a gazetteer and returns a point, which locates the subject.

The harder form, monoplotting, marks the same features in the image and on a 3D terrain model and solves for the position the scene was rendered from — with direction, height and field of view. That locates the viewpoint, and reports how badly the image resisted being fitted at all.

Module 4 works both. See also meisho.

Gō 号

An art name. An Edo print designer worked under one and changed it, sometimes several times across a career, to mark a new stage of work or on inheriting a studio.

The man catalogued as Hokusai signed as Shunrō, Sōri, Taito, Iitsu and Gakyō Rōjin Manji at different points. All are him. A search on one name returns part of a body of work.

Meisho 名所

A famous place: a site with an established literary and pictorial reputation, depicted according to conventions built up over centuries rather than from direct observation.

This matters the moment prints are put on a map. A meisho view records where a place sits in a tradition of depiction, which is a different fact from where it sits on the ground.

NDL 国立国会図書館

The National Diet Library, Japan's legal deposit library. Its digital collections are extensive and its records are bibliographic: they describe publications.

An NDL record therefore answers questions about edition, extent and publisher well, and questions about attribution and image content thinly. That is the cataloguing tradition working as designed.

Its authority file, Web NDL Authorities, is published separately as linked data: each record has a URI serving RDF/XML, Turtle or JSON-LD, with a SPARQL endpoint alongside. It records the preferred form of a name, the variant forms treated as the same, and increasingly links out to Wikidata.

Colophon 奥付

The block of publication information at the end of a Japanese book: publisher, address, date, sometimes the names of others involved.

It is the book stating its own origins, and it is the first place to look when a record's date has no visible source. A book whose colophon is missing, replaced or reused across editions is exactly the case where records go astray.

Kuzushiji 崩し字

The cursive script in which most pre-modern Japanese texts are written, and which most readers of modern Japanese cannot read without training.

Machine recognition of it exists and is improving, and it is a different problem from OCR on printed roman type. Where a transcription in a record came from a model rather than a reader, it carries a different kind of confidence.

Taught separately; these modules assume no reading knowledge.

Tehon 手本

An early modern practice book, usually illustrated, that taught a learner to make something: how to hold the brush, how a form is built up, what to attempt next.

A tehon assumed you would work through it with materials in front of you rather than read it through. This site takes its name and its method from that.

Censor seal 改印

A small stamped mark showing that a design passed official approval before printing. The systems changed several times through the Edo period, and the form of the seal is therefore datable.

This makes it one of the few dating claims that the sheet itself carries, as distinct from a date a cataloguer inferred. Where a record gives a date and the sheet gives a seal, the seal is the stronger evidence.

Union catalogue

A catalogue organised around works rather than holdings, so the variant titles a book has been known by cluster onto one record. A library catalogue asks what this institution holds; a union catalogue asks what this book is.

That difference is why a title search can fail in every museum catalogue and succeed here. For early modern Japanese books the union catalogue of Japanese texts at the National Institute of Japanese Literature is the usual starting point when a title search has failed.

The same move an authority file makes for names, applied to titles.

Ukiyo-e 浮世絵

Pictures of the floating world: the commercial print and painting culture of Edo-period Japan, covering actors, courtesans, landscapes, famous places, warriors and much else.

The word names a market and a milieu rather than a style, which is why collections that treat it as a genre label end up with records that are hard to compare.

Publisher 版元

The hanmoto commissioned the design, owned the blocks, financed the printing and carried the commercial risk. Publishers' marks appear on the sheet and are datable, since firms moved address, changed marks and were inherited.

Blocks were also sold on. A set of blocks could be reissued decades later by a different firm, under a different title, which is how one book comes to have six.

ARC 立命館大学アート・リサーチセンター

The Art Research Center at Ritsumeikan University, Kyoto. A specialist database of Japanese prints and illustrated books, built by researchers rather than by a holding institution, and the largest of its kind.

Because it was built for research on prints, it records things a general museum system has no field for: publisher marks, censor seals, states. Its conventions are those of Japanese print scholarship, which is worth knowing before comparing it with a Western museum record.

Ehon 絵本

A picture book: a woodblock-printed book in which the images carry the work. The category covers albums of prints, drawing manuals, illustrated verse anthologies and natural-history books.

Cataloguing struggles with it, because an ehon is simultaneously a publication with an edition and a set of images with a designer. Library and museum systems each capture one of those well.

The computational operation

The formats, methods and infrastructures, and what each one commits you to.

Aggregator

A service that gathers records from many institutions into one search. Japan Search and Europeana are the large examples.

An aggregator inherits its fields from its contributors, so a claim found there is not independent confirmation of the same claim found at the contributing institution. It is the same claim, seen twice.

Annotation

A note bound to a region of an image rather than placed beside it. The IIIF and W3C annotation models make the binding explicit — this text is about that rectangle on that canvas — so the note travels with the image, can be exported, and can be cited.

Most display tools put text next to a picture and leave the joining to the reader. For material where what matters is positional — a seal in a corner, a signature at an edge, a line of verse on the facing leaf — that joining is exactly the work a reader new to the material cannot do.

Module 7 treats this as one of two axes on which a display tool can be placed.

API 応用プログラム interface

A door in the side of a database, made for programs rather than people. The reading room has a public entrance where you fill in a slip and a staff entrance where the catalogue is queried directly. An API is the second one.

When a module fetches a record without opening a web page, it is using an API. When an institution changes one, every tool built on it stops working, which is why this site caches its data.

Authority file

An agreed list of names, each with one preferred form and an identifier. It exists because Utamaro appears in catalogues as Kitagawa Utamaro, Utamaro I, 喜多川歌麿 and half a dozen romanisations, and something has to decide that these are one person.

Familiar in a different guise: an authority file is what a well-run index does, formalised so that a machine can follow it.

Used heavily in the spring seminar. See also record.

Cached data

A copy of a file kept locally so that the work continues when the original becomes unreachable. Every module here ships with cached data in data/, and every cached file records where it came from, when it was captured, and what its licence permits.

The reasoning is the same reasoning you apply to a citation: a claim that depends on something nobody else can reach is a weak claim.

Canvas

One view in a digitised object: usually one leaf, sometimes one opening. The word is unfortunate. It comes from IIIF's design, where each view is a blank surface onto which images are placed, and it carries no implication about painting.

Counting canvases gives you the digitised extent, which is a different quantity from the extent of the book. Covers, colour charts and rulers all get canvases.

Cell

One block in a notebook. Text cells hold prose; code cells hold code and, once run, their output. A notebook is a stack of cells read top to bottom.

CIDOC-CRM and CRMinf

The museum sector's shared vocabulary for cultural heritage data. Its organising move is to describe events rather than objects: a production, with participants in distinct roles, at a time, in a place. That costs several more statements per claim and lets you say that four people made a print.

CRMinf is its extension for argumentation, supplying classes for belief, inference and premise — so an assertion can be recorded as held by someone, on stated grounds, at a given strength. Most vocabularies have no equivalent, which is why provenance of claims is usually improvised.

Module 3 weighs it against schema.org. See also ResearchSpace.

ResearchSpace

An open-source platform built at the British Museum around a single idea: that a research assertion is a thing in its own right, made by someone, on a stated basis, which others can strengthen or weaken.

An assertion there can rest on direct observation, on adopting another scholar's belief, or on inference from premises held in the system — and it carries that provenance with it. Reasoning is modelled as adjusting confidence in a hypothesis rather than as proving a fact, which is closer to how an art-historical argument actually runs.

Built on CIDOC-CRM with CRMinf. Worth reading before designing your own provenance fields; adopting it is a separate decision, since the cost is a platform rather than a file.

A running instance covers exactly the material of these modules: Late Hokusai, holding the artist's late work including the 103 rediscovered drawings, across the British Museum, the Metropolitan Museum and the Freer|Sackler.

Collections as data

The practice of publishing a collection as a downloadable dataset rather than only as pages to browse. A museum that offers its whole catalogue as one file treats its collection as data; a museum that offers only a search box does not.

The distinction matters because the second arrangement lets you ask about one object at a time and the first lets you ask about the collection.

Commit

GitHub's word for saving, with a short message attached saying what changed. The accumulated messages become a history of the work, which is the point.

Write the message plainly. Future-you is the audience.

CSV

Comma-separated values: a table stored as plain text, one row per line, columns divided by commas. It opens in Excel, in a text editor, and in any programming language, and it will still open in forty years.

This durability is why the modules use it in preference to a spreadsheet file.

Dictionary

In Python, a set of labelled values that you look things up in by name: manifest["label"] asks for the value stored under label. A loaded JSON file becomes one of these.

It behaves like a reference book rather than a list — you go to the entry you want instead of counting through from the start.

See also key and value.

DOI

A permanent identifier for a published thing, which resolves to wherever that thing currently lives. Journals have used them for decades; datasets, software and teaching resources can have them too.

Each released version of this site gets one through Zenodo, so a citation keeps working after the domain stops.

Encoding

How text is stored as numbers. Modern files use Unicode, which covers Japanese, and older Japanese files often use Shift-JIS, which does not travel.

The symptom of getting it wrong is a column of 文字化け — text arriving as boxes or nonsense. The fix is to tell the program which encoding the file uses, rather than letting it guess.

Endpoint

The particular address an API answers at. Institutions move them, retire them and rename them, usually without announcement.

A module that hard-codes an endpoint has a lifespan. A module that ships with cached data does not.

Field

One labelled slot in a record: Date, Creator, Call Number. Fields look alike and are not alike. Some hold facts checkable by anyone who visits the building; others hold conclusions reached by argument.

Digital display gives both the same typeface and the same row height. Recovering the difference is what these modules are for.

IIIF

International Image Interoperability Framework, said "triple-eye-eff". An agreement among libraries and museums about how to publish digitised objects so that one viewer can display material from many institutions.

What it gives you in practice: the ability to put a copy at Waseda beside a copy at the Met in the same window, and a manifest for each that you can read directly.

JSON

A way of writing labelled values and nested lists in plain text, readable by a person and by a machine. Almost every manifest and most museum APIs use it.

Opening one for the first time looks forbidding: brackets, quotation marks, colons. It is a list of labels and their values, indented to show what sits inside what. Nothing more.

Key and value

The two halves of an entry. In "label": "Ehon mushi erabi" the key is label and the value is the title. You ask for a key and receive its value.

The keys are chosen by whoever wrote the file, which is why two institutions describing the same book can share almost no key names.

Manifest

A single file, in JSON, describing one digitised object: how many views, in what order, with what description, under what rights. A viewer reads it before showing you anything.

It is the institution's account of its own copy, written down in a form you can read directly rather than through an interface that has already made choices about what to emphasise.

Markdown

A convention for writing formatted text in a plain file: ## at the start of a line makes a heading, asterisks make italics. The text cells of a notebook use it, and so does this site's README.

It stays readable when the software that renders it disappears, which is the whole argument for it.

Metadata

Description of a thing, as distinct from the thing. A catalogue card is metadata; the book is not.

The word suggests neutrality and objectivity. Every field in a record was entered by somebody, from something, and the record almost never says what that something was.

Notebook

A document of prose and runnable code interleaved, read top to bottom. The file extension is .ipynb. It behaves like an essay with working steps in it, and that is the right way to read one.

These open in Colab, which runs them on Google's machine so that nothing installs on yours.

See also cell.

OCR

Optical character recognition: software reading text out of an image. It works well on printed roman type and much less well on woodblock-printed Japanese, where the equivalent problem is reading kuzushiji and needs models trained for it.

Where a record's transcription came from OCR, it carries the confidence of a machine rather than a reader. That is a different kind of claim.

Opening 見開き

Two facing leaves seen together. In an early modern Japanese illustrated book the opening, rather than the page, is the composed surface: images routinely run across the gutter, and a design read one leaf at a time is a design cut in half.

Most viewers show single pages by default, and do so tidily enough that the loss is easy to miss.

Module 7 makes this the first of six conditions.

Record

One catalogue entry: a set of fields and their values, describing one object in one institution.

Four institutions holding the same book produce four records that disagree, and the disagreements are informative. Module 2 puts them side by side.

Repository

On GitHub, a folder of files with a permanent public address and a full history of changes. This site is one.

The word also means a holding institution, and both senses appear in the same manifest. Where a record says Repository it means the library.

Rights statement

A short field saying what may be done with a digitised image. Two kinds turn up and they are not equivalent.

A rightsstatements.org code describes copyright status and grants nothing. A Creative Commons licence grants permission. An empty field states no position, which you should read as closed.

An Edo-period book is long out of copyright as a work; the photograph of it may carry its own claim.

Runtime

The machine Colab lends you for the session, and the menu that controls it. Runtime → Run all executes every cell in order. Closing the tab gives the machine back.

Schema

The agreed shape of a record: which fields exist, what each is called, what may go in it. Two catalogues using the same schema can be compared directly; two using different schemas require you to decide what corresponds to what.

That deciding is an interpretive act, and it is where most of the argument in a data project actually happens.

URI

An address for a thing. A URL is the familiar kind, pointing at a page you can open. Others point at concepts — a rights statement, a person in an authority file — and exist so that two records can refer to the same idea without relying on spelling.

Viewer

The interface an institution wraps around a digitised object: the page-turning window with a zoom control. It reads a manifest and makes display decisions from it.

A viewer is persuasive. It presents one copy with a smoothness that suggests completeness, and it shows a contested attribution in the same type as a shelfmark.

It also brings assumptions about what a book is — page as unit, left to right, image inside a text block — which arrive with the software rather than being chosen. Module 7 holds them against what this material requires.

Wikidata and Wikibase

An openly editable knowledge base and the hub most other datasets point at, which makes a Wikidata identifier the cheapest way for a name to travel between projects.

What may be added is governed by a notability rule: an item needs a sitelink to a Wikimedia page, or serious publicly available references, or a structural need — being required to make statements on another item more useful. For early modern Japanese material this bites at exactly the point where the field is most interesting, since block cutters, printers and individual prints rarely clear the first condition and depend on external identifiers to clear the second.

Wikibase is the same software run as your own instance, with no notability rule, no community and no incoming links. Where your data will not be accepted, this is the project's own suggested alternative, and the trade it represents is the subject of module 3.

Because items can be merged, revised or deleted by others, record the date you reconciled alongside the identifier.

viewingDirection and behavior

The two fields in a manifest that tell a viewer which way a sequence runs and how to group it. viewingDirection takes right-to-left for this material; the behaviour field takes paged for a book shown as openings, continuous for a scroll, individuals for unrelated sheets, and single canvases can be marked non-paged.

Where they are absent a viewer applies its own defaults, which are almost always left to right and single pages. Nothing errors; the book is displayed backwards. An absent viewingDirection means an institution has published a book without saying which way it reads.

Zenodo

An archive run by CERN that takes a copy of a repository each time a version is released and issues a DOI for it.

It is the mechanism that turns a website into something citable, and something that survives the disappearance of the website.
